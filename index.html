Unequal Time

Commute Inequality Between the Bronx and Manhattan

Balla Sy · QSS 45 · Dartmouth College

Main result

11.83 minutes

The average Bronx census tract has a substantially longer commute than the average Manhattan tract.

Research Question

Do Bronx census tracts have longer average commute times than Manhattan census tracts?

I also ask how much of the difference is associated with public-transit use, median household income, poverty, unemployment, and working from home.

The project is motivated by spatial mismatch and transport-disadvantage theories. The basic idea is that where people live can shape how easily they can reach employment and other opportunities.

Data and Methods

602 Census tracts
327 Bronx tracts
275 Manhattan tracts

I use 2015 American Community Survey five-year census-tract estimates. The outcome is mean travel time to work in minutes.

For inference, I estimate a borough-only OLS model and a full OLS model with HC3 robust standard errors. For prediction, I compare Linear Regression with Gradient Boosting using the same 80/20 train-test split.

Controls: public-transit use, median household income, poverty, unemployment, and work-at-home rate.

Transit use and socioeconomic characteristics explain a meaningful part of the initial difference, but most of the borough gap remains after adjustment.

This does not establish a causal effect of living in the Bronx. The remaining coefficient should be interpreted as an adjusted geographic disparity that may reflect unmeasured spatial factors such as job location, subway-line geometry, transfers, reliability, and land use.

Gradient Boosting performs better out of sample, but the improvement is modest. Test R² increases by 0.020 and RMSE falls by 0.171 minutes.

Interpretation: nonlinearities and interactions add some predictive value, but the linear model already captures most of the available signal.

Transit Use Is Not the Same as Accessibility

≈59% Bronx public-transit use
≈58% Manhattan public-transit use
11.83 min Raw commute difference

The two boroughs have similarly high levels of transit use but very different commute times. That distinction is central to the project.

Transit ridership measures whether people use public transportation. Accessibility asks what jobs and opportunities people can actually reach within a reasonable amount of time.

Conclusion

Commute inequality in New York is not simply a question of whether transit exists. It is a question of what transit allows people to reach, and how long reaching it takes.

Bronx census tracts have a large commute-time disadvantage relative to Manhattan. Socioeconomic and transportation controls reduce the gap by 22.5 percent, but a 9.17-minute adjusted difference remains.

The next step is to measure actual accessibility using MTA network travel times, transfers, service frequency, job locations, and the number of jobs reachable within 30, 45, or 60 minutes.
